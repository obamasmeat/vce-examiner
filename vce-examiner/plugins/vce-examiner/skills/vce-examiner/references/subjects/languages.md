# VCE Languages (shared guide for all language studies)

Sources: 2025 written examination reports for French, Japanese Second Language and Chinese Second Language, used as representative examples. Most VCE language written exams share this structure. Check the specific language's specifications for word/character limits and section details. My own summary.

## Typical written exam structure
- **Section 1 – Listening and reading (responses in English):** understand and convey general and specific information.
- **Section 2 – Listening/reading, responding in the target language:** convey relevant information from the texts, plus a short written task using a stimulus (often with a visual), for a set audience, style and text type, with a shorter word/character limit.
- **Section 3 – Writing in the target language:** one task from several (persuasive, informative, personal/reflective, imaginative), each with a prescribed **text type** and **writing style**, and a word/character range (e.g. 400–500 ji for Japanese SL).
- Section 2 and 3 tasks are marked **holistically** against published criteria. Content and language aren't marked separately. There's also an oral exam, which this skill can't hear but can help prepare for.

## Comprehension (Sections 1–2): how marks are lost
- **Key details omitted**: intensifiers and qualifiers ("very", "various", "happily", "during the summer", "for tourist sites"), dates when the question says "outline the changes", and *who* did what to whom.
- **Mistranslating key verbs or terms** (e.g. rent vs buy, support whom, noise pollution rendered loosely, confusing near-homophones or similar characters such as 道/町 and 理念/里面).
- Including **wrong extra information** alongside right answers.
- Too much irrelevant detail instead of the main points asked for.
- Expected kanji or characters should be used in target-language answers. Spelling matters where it changes the word (médecine ≠ médecin).

## Section 2 writing task
- Integrate **several (often five or more) relevant points from the stimulus**, *including the visual*, and adapt them to the task's purpose, audience and the **personal circumstances** the task asks for.
- Don't just paraphrase the stimulus. Express a position or recommendation as the task requires.
- Stay within the shorter limit. Top marks don't need more words.

## Section 3 extended writing
- **Text type conventions are assessed**: emails need an opening and closing, blogs need a date, headings and subheadings, journals need dated personal reflection with depth of feeling, speeches need audience address, formal letters need register.
- **Writing style** fits the task (persuasive, informative, evaluative, imaginative, personal). Keep the tone consistent.
- Address **every part of the task** (e.g. both "how to make friends" *and* "how to stay safe". Two positives *and* two negatives *and* an opinion).
- Develop ideas with specific, vivid examples and a logical structure, with paragraphs and linking words.
- **Avoid pre-prepared or memorised pieces** (they're obvious and often off-task). **Avoid idioms**: they aren't rewarded and are often misused.
- Imaginative tasks must link clearly to the given theme or image.
- Accuracy and range of grammar and vocabulary matter, but communication of relevant content comes first.

## How to mark languages in this skill
- Check comprehension answers point by point against the source text if the student provides it. List each missing or mistranslated detail.
- For writing, comment on task fulfilment, text type, style, organisation, and accuracy and range of language. Give corrected versions of erroneous sentences in the target language with a short explanation.
- If you're not confident in the language, say so and limit the feedback to what you can judge reliably.
