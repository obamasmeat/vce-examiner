# VCE English (Study Design from 2024)

Sources: VCAA exam specifications (2025 version), expected qualities descriptors, 2025 and 2024 external assessment reports. Summarised in my own words. These are not official VCAA wording.

## Exam format
- 15 min reading + 3 hours writing. Worth 50% of the study score. Total 60 marks.
- **Section A – Analytical response to a text (20):** one topic, on one of the two List 1 texts studied. Each text has two topics.
- **Section B – Creating a text (20):** one written text (no poetry/song/verse). It must use the **fixed title**, idea(s) from **at least one stimulus**, and connect to **one Framework of Ideas** (country / protest / personal journeys / play). Purpose: to explain, express, reflect or argue.
- **Section C – Analysis of argument and language (20):** analyse how argument, written/spoken language and visuals in an unseen text persuade an intended audience. The student is **not** asked to evaluate whether the argument is good.
- Each section is marked **holistically** against criteria by two assessors (score out of 10 each). The "expected qualities" bands run 9–10, 8, 7, 6, 5, 4, 3, 1–2, 0.

## How the criteria interact (important for marking)
Assessors treat the three expected qualities (EQs) as linked, in this order:
1. **Quality of ideas** in response to *this topic* (Section A) or *this title + stimulus + Framework* (Section B).
2. **How the ideas are structured, developed and backed up.**
3. **Language control.**
Ideas set the ceiling. Fluent writing about the wrong thing can't score highly: language is never rewarded separately from relevance. Quotations that don't serve a relevant discussion earn nothing.

## Section A – what separates bands
**Top band (9–10):** a close, perceptive reading. Grasps the topic's *implications* and complexity, with a cogent, controlled, well-backed discussion in precise language.
**Middle (5–6):** clear knowledge of the text, a response to the topic backed by evidence, generally organised.
**Low (3–4):** familiar with the text but only a limited sense of the topic.

Assessors rewarded:
- **Unpacking the topic.** Absolute words ("no right choices", "inseparable", "primarily"), connotations, the verb (warns / celebrates / mocks / condemns: don't flatten it into "presents"), what the topic leaves unsaid, comparisons ("more than", "more dangerous than"), and any quotation given.
- **Challenging the proposition where justified, but never dismissing the concepts.** You can argue two ideas are *not* "inseparable"; you can't ignore one of them.
- **Relationship topics** (X leads to / causes / despite / is more than Y) need both concepts and the relationship between them.
- **Strategic sequencing:** topic sentences and closing sentences that state the idea and link paragraphs into one developing reading.
- **Evidence from across the whole text,** of many kinds (language, structure, characters, plot, allusion), treated as *interpretation*, not "fact".
- A conceptual, even philosophical, reading rather than a literal one.
- A conclusion that **resolves** the topic, showing the link between the author's context, audience and purpose. Simply restating the argument isn't enough.
- Sophisticated vocabulary used *with purpose*. Polysyllabic words used wrongly hurt.

Common ways students lost marks:
- Reading the topic through a different lens it doesn't invite (e.g. a "patriarchy" essay for a topic about truth).
- Discussing only one of two concepts (villains but not victims).
- Swapping in a more familiar concept, or **twisting the topic** to fit a prepared essay.
- Mixing up the two topics on offer, or misreading or ignoring a qualifying word.
- **Character-by-character or poem-by-poem structure** instead of idea-driven paragraphs.
- Linking words ("initially… later… finally") that imply connections the essay never makes.
- Plot examples presented as the argument, with quotes used only as "proof it happened".
- **Memorised quotations** unrelated to the topic.
- Covering only part of the text (just the opening, or one or two poems).
- Relying on background context or material from outside the text instead of the text itself.
- No conclusion, or a conclusion that only repeats.

## Section B – what separates bands
**Top band:** insightful use of title + stimulus + Framework, a cohesive text with an explicit purpose and fitting voice, sophisticated control of structure and language features.

Rewarded:
- The title used as the conceptual focus (it is fixed and can't be changed). Writing it at the top helps.
- Stimulus ideas woven in, often implicitly, sometimes explicitly, to amplify meaning.
- A voice that deliberately connects with the reader. Trusting an adult reader to infer meaning.
- Cohesive devices: a recurring image or symbol, returning to the opening image at the end, extended metaphor, economical shifts in time and place.
- Mixing purposes (e.g. reflection + explanation) is fine.

Penalised:
- **Literal use of stimulus** with no deeper link to the Framework (e.g. children playing a board game for "play").
- No clear purpose, rambling, simplistic ideas.
- **A pre-written piece contorted** to fit the title.
- Plot alone carrying the ideas, with no visible authorial choices.
- Writing for yourself rather than the reader. Sensationalised content, cloying sentiment, clichés.
- Framework misread (e.g. "protest" taken as a verb rather than the Framework's concept).
- Remember the assessor is an adult stranger: content must be appropriate.

## Section C – what separates bands
**Top band:** perceptive understanding of contention, how the argument *develops*, and point of view. Sophisticated insight into how language and visuals **work together** to persuade the *intended* audience.

Rewarded:
- Establishing **context, audience and purpose** early, and *using* it in the analysis (why this audience, why this appeal works on them).
- Explaining **strategic sequencing**: why the writer orders arguments this way (e.g. shared values first, then problems, then rebuttal, then solution; alternating emotional and practical appeals so the case is harder to dismiss).
- **Integrated** analysis: argument, language and visuals discussed together rather than ticked off separately. Top responses drop the rigid scaffold.
- **Selectivity**: analysing the most powerful elements, not everything in order.
- Explaining *how* an effect is achieved and *why* it works on this reader. Implied meaning.

Penalised:
- **Identifying without analysing** (naming tone, text type or a visual with no explanation). Identification earns nothing.
- **Claiming an effect without explaining it** ("this makes the reader feel shocked").
- Generic analysis of stock devices (inclusive language, rhetorical questions) that would fit any text.
- Overstating impact ("forces the reader to conclude").
- Summarising the stages of the argument without saying why they're ordered that way.
- Visuals described but not linked to how the argument develops.
- One giant paragraph covering half the article because of a rigid three-body-paragraph template.

## Marking notes for this skill
- Give a score out of 10 per section (as one assessor would), naming the band and the descriptor words that fit.
- Always say what the next band would need, in concrete terms.
- Assessors accept exam writing as a timed first draft: minor slips don't sink a response, but expression that blurs meaning does.
